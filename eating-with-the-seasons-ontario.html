A useful place to start

Before choosing something new, notice what is already waiting in your fridge.

Seasonal eating can begin with a small change: trying asparagus when it arrives, using ripe summer tomatoes in a familiar meal, or planning a winter dinner around carrots and beans.

You do not need a completely new menu every week. A useful routine is to notice what is available, choose foods that fit your life, and give the ingredients you buy a clear purpose. Planning around food you already have can help prevent waste. [5]

01 / Read Ontario’s food calendar

What does “in season” mean in Ontario?

A crop’s outdoor harvest is one part of the picture. Ontario also supplies produce from storage and from greenhouses. Foodland Ontario’s calendar tracks availability: it lists apples and carrots year-round, and distinguishes field-grown tomatoes and cucumbers from greenhouse versions. [1]

Use a calendar as a starting point rather than a promise. Weather, growing region, variety and production method affect what reaches a shop or market. For Hamilton-area shopping, ask where an item was grown and what is arriving that week. [2]

Keep fresh, frozen and canned options in the picture

Canada’s food guide recognises all three as healthy choices. Choose frozen produce without added sugars or rich sauces, canned vegetables with little or no added sodium, and canned fruit with little or no added sugar. Seasonal fresh food can sit alongside these options. [3]

03 / Keep a familiar structure

Change the produce, keep the meal idea

Instead of collecting a new recipe for every purchase, keep a few adaptable meals in mind: a grain bowl, vegetable soup, whole grain pasta, a wrap or a stir-fry. Change the vegetables with the season and use a protein food you enjoy.

A helpful guide to proportions

½Vegetables & fruits
¼Whole grain foods
¼Protein foods

Canada’s food guide uses these proportions for meals, including mixed dishes. Choose plant-based protein foods more often. The diagram is a proportion guide, not a fixed serving size or a calorie calculation. [4]

For a bowl, that might mean plenty of vegetables, some brown rice or barley, and beans, lentils, tofu, fish or another protein food. For soup, think about the mix in the pot and what you serve alongside it. Adapt the flavours to your household’s food traditions and preferences.

Make a shopping list with room to change

Write “a vegetable for roasting” or “fruit for breakfasts”, then choose an option that fits your budget and meals. Check the fridge, freezer and pantry first. Buy for the number of meals you will actually prepare, and keep a convenient frozen option for a busy evening. [5]

04 / Give ingredients more than one use

Two meals and a freezer portion

Start with a small autumn plan: carrots, winter squash, cooked lentils and a whole grain. Decide how much you need before cooking; a household of one may want a much smaller batch.

  1. Today: make a grain bowl

    Roast the vegetables and serve a portion with lentils and a whole grain. Before serving, set aside separate portions for tomorrow and the freezer.

  2. Tomorrow: change the format

    Use the properly chilled reserved vegetables and lentils in a whole grain wrap. Add a fresh vegetable you already have, rather than buying another bag just for this meal.

  3. Later: use the portion you froze

    Freeze the extra portion promptly after cooling. Label it with its contents and cooking date. It can become the base of a future soup, with extra vegetables and liquid when you prepare it.

Adapt the amounts to your household. Canada’s food guide shows how prepared vegetables, legumes and whole grains can be used in different meals. [6]

Keep the food safe as well as useful

Cool cooked food promptly in shallow containers; do not leave it out for more than two hours. Refrigerate at 4°C or colder, and use refrigerated leftovers within 2–3 days or freeze them. Keep the original cooking date on the label. [8][9]

Thaw frozen leftovers in the fridge or microwave and use them immediately after thawing. If reheating, heat to 74°C throughout and check with a food thermometer. Reheat only what you need; discard uneaten reheated leftovers. [9]

05 / Help good food last

Store each food where it belongs

Getting food home is only the first step. Follow package directions and use storage that suits the item; not every vegetable or fruit belongs in the same drawer.

Berries & apples

Refrigerate in well-ventilated containers or bags. Rinse berries when preparing or eating them, rather than putting them away wet.

Carrots & beets

Remove leafy tops before refrigerating. Their leaves spoil faster than the roots.

Onions & garlic

Keep dry bulb onions at room temperature. Store garlic somewhere cool, dry and well ventilated.

Cut & prepared produce

Refrigerate promptly. Store raw meat and its juices separately from produce and ready-to-eat food.

Storage examples: Health Canada’s vegetable and fruit guide and safe food storage guidance. Keep the fridge at 4°C or colder and freezer at −18°C or colder. [7][8]

Freezing can help when you have more sound produce than you can use. Follow the crop’s preparation instructions: most vegetables need blanching before freezing to slow enzymes that affect quality. The timing depends on the vegetable and its size; use a tested crop-specific guide. [10]

06 / Build a routine you can keep

Notice, use, then adjust

Make the next useful action easy to see. A small “use first” area in the fridge can hold food that needs attention soon. Keep date labels visible and check it before making another shopping list.

  • Buy with a purpose. Give each fresh purchase a meal or snack. A large pack only helps if you can use or safely store it.
  • Make a plan for the remainder. If a recipe uses part of a cabbage, choose another meal for the rest while you are planning.
  • Freeze early enough to help. Set aside food within its safe storage window, then label the portion. Choose food you have handled and stored safely. [8][9]
  • Learn from one week. Notice what repeatedly goes uneaten. Buy less of it next time, try a different format, or choose something your household prefers.

These are practical ways to apply the food guide’s advice to plan purchases, use overlapping ingredients and reuse prepared food. Choose one habit first, rather than trying to reorganise your whole kitchen. [5][6]

A Hamilton connection

Give unavoidable scraps the right destination

Check the City of Hamilton’s Green Bin guidance for food scraps you cannot use. Keeping edible food in meals is the first goal; local organics collection provides a route for accepted scraps. [12]

07 / Common questions

A flexible approach, all year

Do I have to buy only fresh or Ontario-grown food?

No. Use seasonal Ontario produce when it fits your meals, access and budget. Frozen and canned vegetables and fruit can be healthy, convenient choices. A workable routine can include all of them. [3]

Does “in season” guarantee a lower price?

No price is guaranteed by this calendar. Compare the prices and package sizes in front of you, then consider how much your household will actually use. A smaller purchase may be more useful than a larger quantity that goes uneaten.

Should I throw food away on its best-before date?

A Canadian best-before date describes the quality of an unopened, properly stored food; it is not a safety deadline or a safety guarantee. Once opened, storage life can change. Follow storage instructions, and do not confuse this with an expiration date, which applies to certain products and must be respected. [11]

Can I judge leftover safety by smell?

No. Harmful contamination may not change a food’s appearance, smell or taste. Use safe handling, temperatures and storage times instead. Do not taste questionable food to decide whether to keep it. [8][9]

Let the season offer an idea, then let your kitchen shape the plan. Choosing a few foods you enjoy, storing them well and using them fully is a practical place to begin.
